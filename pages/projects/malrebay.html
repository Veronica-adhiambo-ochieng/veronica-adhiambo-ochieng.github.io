Overview

MalReBay is an R package that uses parasite genetic data to tell us how well antimalarial drugs are working, so drug resistance can be spotted early. It started as my master's thesis at AIMS Rwanda and I now lead its development at Swiss TPH, with Dr. Monica Golumbeanu.

Why it matters

To catch drug resistance early, we need to know when a malaria drug is failing. In drug trials, the infection sometimes comes back after treatment. Researchers then have to decide: is it the same infection coming back, which means the drug failed (a recrudescence), or a brand-new infection from another mosquito bite? They decide by comparing the parasite's genes before and after treatment.

The gap

The method most studies use today is match counting: compare a handful of genetic markers and count how many match. It has two problems:

  • It is sensitive to missing data. Genotyping often misses some markers, and one missing marker can flip the call.
  • It can't express uncertainty. It gives a yes or a no, even when the evidence is unclear, for example when a patient carries several parasite strains at once.

When the call is wrong, a drug can look better or worse than it really is. What's missing is a probabilistic approach.

What MalReBay does

MalReBay fills that gap with a Bayesian algorithm. It accounts for missing markers and for patients carrying several strains, and instead of a yes or no it estimates the probability that each returning infection is a recrudescence. Those probabilities give more reliable estimates of how well a drug works, and of where resistance may be emerging.

It builds on the original Bayesian algorithm developed by Dr. Mateusz Plucinski at the US CDC, and turns that idea into a package researchers can install and use.

What I did

  • Led the design and development of the package, building on my master's thesis.
  • Extended the original framework, built for microsatellite markers, to MSP/GLURP and amplicon sequencing data.
  • Made the code reproducible and maintainable, and wrote documentation, tutorials and examples.
  • With my supervisor, reviewed existing methods (Aster and PfRecur) and compared them with MalReBay, including how each one copes as more genetic information goes missing.
  • Built an interactive dashboard for exploring MalReBay's results.
  • Review collaborators' code on GitHub before it is merged.

Tools

  • R: the package itself.
  • Bayesian inference and Stan: estimating the probability behind each classification, sampled with Hamiltonian Monte Carlo.
  • Git and GitHub: version control and code review with collaborators.

Where it stands

  • The package is publicly available.
  • The comparison with existing methods is ongoing.
  • I'm writing the methods manuscript as first author: Ochieng, V. A., Golumbeanu, M., Plucinski, M. M., and Nsanzabana, C. (2026). A Bayesian algorithm for molecular correction in malaria therapeutic efficacy studies. Manuscript in preparation.
  • I presented the thesis research behind MalReBay at a National Malaria Control Programme capacity-building workshop in Kenya in August 2025.
